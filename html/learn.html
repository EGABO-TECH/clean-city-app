Types of Waste

Uganda generates several types of waste. Each requires different handling and disposal methods.

Waste Segregation

Separating waste at the source makes recycling easier and reduces the burden on landfills.

Recycling

Recycling converts waste materials into new products, creating jobs and reducing pollution.

Composting

Organic waste like food scraps and garden trimmings can become nutrient-rich compost for farming.

Hazardous Waste

Medical, chemical, and electronic waste require special handling. Never mix them with regular waste.

Environmental Impact

Improper waste disposal pollutes soil, water, and air. Proper management protects Uganda's ecosystems.

The 3 Rs of Waste Management

Reduce, Reuse, Recycle - the foundation of sustainable waste management.

Reduce

Buy less, choose products with minimal packaging, and avoid single-use plastics. The best waste is the waste we never create.

Reuse

Repurpose containers, donate items, repair instead of replace. Reusing extends product life and saves resources.

Recycle

Sort and recycle paper, plastic, glass, and metal. Recycling saves energy, reduces pollution, and creates green jobs.

How to Segregate Waste at Home

A simple guide to sorting your waste properly.

Plastics

Bottles, bags, containers - rinse and place in the blue bin.

Organics

Food scraps, leaves, garden waste - green bin for composting.

Paper

Newspapers, cardboard, office paper - keep dry in the yellow bin.

Hazardous

Batteries, chemicals, medical - separate and call UNWM.

Types of Waste

Uganda generates a diverse range of waste streams. Understanding these categories is the first step toward proper management.

Household Waste

Also known as municipal solid waste, this includes everyday items discarded by homes - food scraps, packaging, paper, plastics, and broken items. It forms the largest portion of waste in Ugandan cities.

Commercial Waste

Generated by businesses such as markets, shops, hotels, and offices. Often similar to household waste but produced in larger volumes and with more packaging materials.

Industrial Waste

Produced by manufacturing and processing plants. May include chemicals, metals, solvents, and other by-products. Requires careful handling and often specialized disposal.

Medical Waste

Generated by hospitals, clinics, and pharmacies. Includes used needles, bandages, expired medicines, and biological materials. Extremely hazardous and must be incinerated or treated.

Electronic Waste (E-Waste)

Discarded computers, phones, TVs, and appliances. Contains valuable metals but also toxic substances like lead and mercury. Requires specialized recycling.

Agricultural Waste

Includes crop residues, animal manure, and agrochemical containers. Can often be composted or converted into energy.

Did you know? Kampala alone generates over 2,000 tonnes of waste every day. Proper segregation at the source can divert up to 60% of this from landfills.

Waste Segregation

Waste segregation is the practice of separating waste into different categories at the point of generation. It is the single most effective way to improve recycling rates.

The Color-Coding System

  • Blue Bin: Dry recyclables - plastics, paper, cardboard, glass, metals.
  • Green Bin: Organic waste - food scraps, garden waste, leaves.
  • Yellow Bin: Paper and cardboard (sometimes separate for cleanliness).
  • Red Bin: Hazardous waste - batteries, chemicals, medical waste.
  • Black/Grey Bin: General non-recyclable waste.

Benefits of Segregation

  • Increases the quality and quantity of recyclables.
  • Reduces the amount of waste sent to landfills.
  • Lowers greenhouse gas emissions from decomposing organic waste.
  • Creates cleaner composting streams.
  • Reduces collection and sorting costs.

Practical Tips

  • Rinse containers before recycling to avoid contamination.
  • Keep a small bin in each room for easy sorting.
  • Label your bins clearly for family members and visitors.
  • Compost organic waste if you have a garden.
Remember: If you mix recyclables with general waste, the entire batch often ends up in a landfill. Segregation starts with you.

Recycling

Recycling is the process of collecting and processing materials that would otherwise be thrown away as trash and turning them into new products.

How Recycling Works

  1. Collection: Recyclables are picked up from homes, businesses, or drop-off centers.
  2. Sorting: Materials are sorted by type (plastic, paper, glass, metal).
  3. Processing: Sorted materials are cleaned, shredded, and melted or pulped.
  4. Manufacturing: Processed materials become raw material for new products.
  5. Purchase: Consumers buy products made from recycled content, completing the loop.

What Can Be Recycled?

  • Plastics: PET bottles, HDPE containers, plastic bags (where facilities exist).
  • Paper: Newspapers, magazines, office paper, cardboard.
  • Glass: Bottles and jars (clear, green, brown).
  • Metals: Aluminum cans, steel cans, scrap metal.

Recycling in Uganda

Uganda's recycling industry is growing. Informal waste pickers, known as "bayaye," collect a significant portion of recyclables. Formal recycling companies are now emerging, focusing on plastics and metals. However, only about 10-15% of waste is currently recycled nationwide - a huge opportunity for growth.

Environmental Impact: Recycling one aluminum can saves enough energy to power a TV for 3 hours. Recycling one tonne of paper saves 17 trees.

Composting

Composting is the natural process of recycling organic matter, such as leaves and food scraps, into a valuable fertilizer that can enrich soil and plants.

What to Compost

  • Greens (Nitrogen-rich): Fruit and vegetable scraps, coffee grounds, grass clippings, plant trimmings.
  • Browns (Carbon-rich): Dry leaves, straw, cardboard, sawdust, paper.
  • Water: Maintain moisture like a wrung-out sponge.
  • Air: Turn the pile regularly to aerate.

What NOT to Compost

  • Meat, fish, and dairy products (attract pests, cause odors).
  • Pet waste (may contain harmful pathogens).
  • Diseased plants (can spread disease).
  • Chemically treated wood or coal ash.

Benefits of Composting

  • Reduces waste sent to landfills.
  • Produces free, nutrient-rich fertilizer.
  • Improves soil structure and water retention.
  • Reduces methane emissions from landfills.
  • Supports sustainable agriculture.
Quick Start: Find a dry, shady spot. Alternate layers of greens and browns. Keep it moist and turn it every 1-2 weeks. In 2-3 months, you'll have rich compost!

Hazardous Waste

Hazardous waste is any waste that poses a substantial threat to human health or the environment. It requires special handling, treatment, and disposal.

Types of Hazardous Waste

  • Medical Waste: Needles, syringes, bandages, expired medicines, human tissue.
  • Chemical Waste: Pesticides, solvents, acids, paints, cleaning agents.
  • Electronic Waste: Batteries, circuit boards, monitors, fluorescent bulbs.
  • Industrial Waste: Heavy metals, oils, sludges from manufacturing.

Safe Handling Guidelines

  • Never place hazardous waste in regular bins.
  • Keep items in their original containers if possible.
  • Do not mix different types of hazardous waste.
  • Wear gloves and masks when handling.
  • Contact UNWM for proper collection and disposal.

Emergency Contacts

For urgent hazardous waste disposal, call our 24/7 emergency line: +256 772 999 000.

Warning: Improper disposal of hazardous waste can contaminate soil, water sources, and cause serious health problems. Always seek professional help.

Environmental Impact of Waste

Improper waste management has far-reaching consequences for Uganda's environment, economy, and public health.

Water Pollution

Waste dumped in rivers, lakes, and wetlands contaminates water sources. Plastics block drainage channels, causing flooding. Chemicals leach into groundwater, making it unsafe for drinking.

Soil Contamination

Toxic substances from waste seep into the soil, killing beneficial microorganisms and reducing agricultural productivity. Heavy metals can accumulate in crops, entering the food chain.

Air Pollution

Open burning of waste releases toxic fumes, including dioxins and furans. Decomposing organic waste in landfills produces methane, a potent greenhouse gas contributing to climate change.

Harm to Wildlife

Plastic waste kills fish, birds, and animals that mistake it for food. Uganda's lakes and national parks are increasingly threatened by plastic pollution.

Public Health Risks

Uncollected waste breeds disease vectors like mosquitoes, flies, and rodents. These spread malaria, typhoid, cholera, and other diseases. Children are especially vulnerable.

Economic Costs

Poor waste management leads to flooding, disease outbreaks, and lost tourism revenue. Cleanup costs are often higher than proper disposal would have been.

Our Shared Responsibility: Every Ugandan can contribute to reducing environmental impact. By segregating waste, composting organic matter, and recycling, we protect our nation's beauty for future generations.
